The Water Cycle

The water cycle, also known as the hydrologic cycle, describes the continuous movement of water on, above, and below the surface of the Earth. Water is constantly recycled through this cycle, and the total amount of water on Earth remains essentially constant.

The water cycle is driven by energy from the Sun and the force of gravity. It consists of several key processes.

Evaporation is the process by which water changes from a liquid to a gas, or water vapor. The Sun heats water in oceans, lakes, and rivers, causing it to evaporate and rise into the atmosphere. A related process, transpiration, is the release of water vapor from plants through their leaves. Together these are sometimes called evapotranspiration.

Condensation occurs when water vapor in the atmosphere cools and changes back into tiny liquid droplets, forming clouds and fog. As these droplets collide and combine, they grow larger.

Precipitation happens when water droplets in clouds become heavy enough to fall back to Earth as rain, snow, sleet, or hail. Precipitation is the primary way that water returns from the atmosphere to the surface.

Collection, or runoff, occurs when precipitation flows over the land into rivers, lakes, and oceans, or soaks into the ground to become groundwater. This water is eventually evaporated again, continuing the cycle.

The water cycle plays a vital role in regulating Earth's climate, distributing fresh water, shaping landscapes through erosion, and supporting all living organisms. Human activities such as deforestation and the burning of fossil fuels can disrupt the natural balance of the water cycle.
