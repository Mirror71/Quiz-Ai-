Photosynthesis

Photosynthesis is the biological process by which green plants, algae, and some bacteria convert light energy into chemical energy stored in glucose. It is the foundation of most food chains on Earth and is responsible for producing the oxygen in the atmosphere.

The overall chemical equation for photosynthesis is:
6 CO2 + 6 H2O + light energy -> C6H12O6 + 6 O2

Photosynthesis takes place mainly in the leaves, inside organelles called chloroplasts. Chloroplasts contain a green pigment called chlorophyll, which absorbs light most strongly in the blue and red regions of the spectrum and reflects green light, giving plants their color.

Photosynthesis occurs in two main stages. The first stage is the light-dependent reactions, which take place in the thylakoid membranes of the chloroplast. Here, light energy is used to split water molecules (a process called photolysis), releasing oxygen as a byproduct. This stage produces the energy carriers ATP and NADPH.

The second stage is the light-independent reactions, also known as the Calvin cycle, which take place in the stroma of the chloroplast. In the Calvin cycle, the ATP and NADPH produced in the first stage are used to convert carbon dioxide into glucose through a series of enzyme-controlled steps. The key enzyme involved is RuBisCO.

Several factors affect the rate of photosynthesis, including light intensity, carbon dioxide concentration, and temperature. When any one of these factors is in short supply, it becomes the limiting factor that restricts the rate of the process.

Photosynthesis is critically important because it provides the energy and oxygen that sustain nearly all aerobic life, and it removes carbon dioxide from the atmosphere, helping to regulate the global climate.
