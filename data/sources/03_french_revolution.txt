The French Revolution

The French Revolution was a period of radical political and social change in France that began in 1789 and lasted until 1799. It fundamentally transformed French society and had a lasting impact on the history of the modern world.

Several causes led to the revolution. France faced a severe financial crisis caused by costly wars, including support for the American Revolution, and the lavish spending of the monarchy. The social structure, known as the Ancien Regime, divided society into three estates: the clergy (First Estate), the nobility (Second Estate), and the common people (Third Estate). The first two estates enjoyed privileges and paid almost no taxes, while the Third Estate, which made up most of the population, carried the tax burden.

The revolution began in 1789. On July 14, 1789, a crowd stormed the Bastille, a royal fortress and prison in Paris, an event that became a symbol of the uprising. In August 1789, the National Assembly adopted the Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen, which proclaimed liberty, equality, and fraternity as fundamental principles.

The monarchy was abolished in 1792, and France was declared a republic. King Louis XVI was executed by guillotine in January 1793. A period known as the Reign of Terror followed, led by Maximilien Robespierre and the Committee of Public Safety, during which thousands of people considered enemies of the revolution were executed.

The revolution ended in 1799 when Napoleon Bonaparte seized power in a coup. The French Revolution spread ideas of nationalism, democracy, and human rights across Europe and inspired later movements for independence and reform around the world.
